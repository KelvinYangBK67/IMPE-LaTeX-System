\chapter*{Preface}
This small book models the structure of a humanities monograph.
Its manuscript witnesses are invented, not historical evidence.
Their purpose is to let the reader inspect chapter openings,
running heads, notes, cross-references, continuous prose and the
transition between preliminary matter and the main text.

A longer document needs room for explanation. Isolated sentences
cannot show how a paragraph behaves over a page break, or how
a reader follows an argument through successive chapters.
The present sample therefore develops one question from a
description of material through textual comparison to an editorial
decision. In a real book, each invented witness would be replaced
with a precisely identified source.

The layout intentionally uses ordinary \LaTeX{} structures.
A writer can replace the prose and section headings without
learning a special markup vocabulary. New features, including
bibliography, illustrations and indexes, can be added when the
actual research calls for them.

\chapter{The Problem of a Text}\label{chap:problem}
\section{What counts as a witness?}
A text is encountered through specific objects: manuscripts,
prints, inscriptions and quotations in other books. These
objects may contain overlapping passages, but they seldom
transmit precisely the same words. Some variations result
from physical damage, others from copying or deliberate
reorganization. The objects are evidence for a textual
history rather than interchangeable containers of words.

Our example introduces three fictional copies, A, B and C.
A is relatively early and gives a short statement; B is
later and gives a longer version; C loses the decisive
middle of the sentence. These properties create a question
rather than a hierarchy. A late manuscript may transmit
an old reading, just as an early witness can contain
an unusual mistake.

Before editing a sentence, the researcher describes each
witness as an object. Where is it preserved? What is its
material condition? Which scribal hands can be identified?
How securely can the proposed date be justified? These
details may seem remote from textual criticism, but an
edition becomes difficult to evaluate when it fails to
connect its readings to physical evidence.

\section{From object to transcription}
A transcription always involves decisions. The scholar
identifies letter shapes, records damage and determines
how to represent additions, deletions and punctuation.
It is useful to distinguish a diplomatic transcription
from a normalized reading text, with explicit conventions
for moving from one form to the other.

Suppose that C has lost the middle of a line. If the editor
silently supplies the wording of A, readers may overlook
the absence of evidence. A responsible transcription
shows the gap, while a possible supplement can appear
in an apparatus or a separate reconstructed text.
That distinction is small typographically but large
methodologically.\footnote{A genuine edition should define
its signs for uncertain letters, gaps and conjectural
supplements, and wherever possible cite a facsimile.}

\section{The limits of chronology}
The oldest surviving witness is not necessarily the
closest copy of an otherwise lost original. A textual
tradition may branch, and copying errors can persist in
one line while a younger copy elsewhere preserves a
more archaic form. The editor needs to compare shared
errors, patterns of revision and the grammar of each
passage before arguing for an earlier reading.

Chapter~\ref{chap:compare} begins only after these
descriptive questions have been stated. This order is
deliberate: first ask what survives and how it survives,
then consider which history explains the differences.
An argument that reverses the order may simply confirm
a preferred reading chosen at the outset.

\chapter{Comparing the Readings}\label{chap:compare}
\section{A small difference}
Imagine that A contains the phrase "the record is kept",
whereas B says "the record is carefully kept". C retains
the opening and closing words, but the middle is damaged.
B's additional word may be an explanatory expansion.
Equally, A may have lost a modifier once present in the
common ancestor of the three copies.

C cannot settle the matter. Its lost area is compatible
with more than one supplement, but compatibility is not
the same as positive attestation. An apparatus that
acknowledges an unresolved question can be more useful
than one that hides the uncertain state of a manuscript.
This is particularly important when a single restored
word controls the interpretation of a longer passage.

\section{Explanations and counterexamples}
The editor can search for parallels in related texts.
If an independent source repeatedly uses the longer
formula, B may deserve greater weight. If a known
scribe frequently inserts explanatory adverbs, the
short form of A may be more economical. Neither
observation should be introduced as a universal rule:
copying practices vary across texts and periods.

An argument is stronger when its author identifies a
possible counterexample. A newly discovered copy
independent of A and B might shift the balance;
another quotation might show that the same phrase
circulated in both forms. The possibility of revision
does not require an editor to suspend judgement
forever, but it does require proportional confidence.

One should also return continually to material details.
Ink traces, overwritten characters and the location
of a gap may settle questions that abstract arguments
about textual economy cannot answer. A history of
transmission gains precision by keeping those
observations separate from the inferences drawn
from them.

\section{Choosing a presentation}
A synoptic edition keeps variants visible in parallel,
making it useful when the development of a textual
tradition is itself the object of research. A reading
edition instead constructs a continuous text and
typically records competing readings in notes.
Both forms can be academically responsible when
their aims and limitations are made explicit.

Page design follows from that choice. Parallel columns
require different widths and annotation strategies
from a continuous single-column text. A book with
extensive manuscript images or dense apparatus
needs a larger or more carefully balanced page.
The present sample demonstrates default running
prose rather than presupposing those extra requirements.

\chapter{Presenting an Edition}\label{chap:edition}
\section{A provisional decision}
For this exercise we provisionally adopt the shorter
reading found in A. This follows a working hypothesis
that B expanded a terse sentence. It is not a proof:
a different editor might prefer B or retain both.
A clear apparatus would record B's addition and
C's damage so that the decision remains reviewable.

The success of an edition depends less on the
confidence of a single reconstructed line than on
the inspectability of its reasoning. Sigla must
be defined, references precise and alternatives
legible. A later scholar should be able to return
to the witnesses without unravelling hidden
decisions embedded in the printed text.

\section{Chapter structure and notes}
Chapter~\ref{chap:problem} describes our objects;
Chapter~\ref{chap:compare} discusses the readings;
the present chapter explains how to present an
editorial result. Cross-references allow readers
to move through that structure rather than
remembering every page number unaided.

Running heads identify the current discussion
when a page is opened in the middle of a chapter.
Section headings indicate smaller turns in the
argument. Footnotes accommodate supporting
information without dissolving the main text
into a list of citations.\footnote{Replace this
illustrative note with a verified source reference
or a concise comment about the textual evidence.}

Typography cannot replace accurate research.
A perfectly aligned table cannot correct a
mistaken transcription, and an elegant heading
cannot establish an unsupported date. Yet
consistent presentation can make the structure
of an argument easier to inspect and challenge,
which is an important scholarly benefit.

\section{Using this source}
Replace the title, the invented sources and
the prose in each chapter. Add further chapters
without changing the document class. Front
matter can include a list of abbreviations,
a dedication or notes on editorial conventions.
A bibliography, index or illustrations can be
enabled when the project actually needs them.

During revision, inspect the typeset PDF as
well as the source. A sentence that looks
balanced in the editor may produce an
unfortunate page break, or a heading may be
isolated from its next paragraph. The aim is
to adjust presentation without changing
the evidence or the hierarchy of argument.

\chapter*{Afterword}
This fictional exercise has served its purpose
if it makes the distinction between evidence,
inference and editorial decision clear.
A sound monograph permits readers to follow
that distinction across chapters, while the
page layout gives each part a recognizable
and comfortable place.

Use the document as a starting point rather
than a prescribed research design. Real
sources, precise citations, specialized
scripts and illustrations should be added
as the inquiry develops, with continued
attention to the readability of the result.
